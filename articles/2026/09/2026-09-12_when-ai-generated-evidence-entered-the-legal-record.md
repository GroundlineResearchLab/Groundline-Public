# When AI-Generated Evidence Entered the Legal Record

**Date:** 12 September 2026

**Domain:** Evidence Systems and Legal Provenance

**Publication Type:** Article

## Context

Artificial intelligence creates more than one problem for legal evidence.

A court may encounter an image that has been enhanced by AI, an audiovisual file alleged to be synthetic, a reconstruction produced from source data, a facial recognition result, or an analytical conclusion generated by a machine learning system. These materials do not share the same evidentiary problem merely because AI is involved.

That distinction is already visible in the institutions responsible for evidence policy.

The U.S. Judicial Conference Advisory Committee on Evidence Rules has identified two evidentiary challenges raised by AI: evidence that is an acknowledged product of AI and evidence that is not authentic because it is a difficult-to-detect deepfake.[1] The National Center for State Courts has similarly distinguished acknowledged AI-generated evidence from material whose AI generation or alteration is alleged but not acknowledged.[2][3]

This is different from another legal AI problem: lawyers or judges using generative AI to draft documents, conduct research, or produce citations that turn out to be false. Those failures concern professional verification, legal research, and filing integrity. They may affect a court record, but they are not the same question as whether an AI-generated or AI-altered item is being offered as evidence of an event, person, condition, relationship, or fact.

The evidence problem examined here is narrower:

**How does AI-generated evidence change the evidence architecture required for material entering the legal record?**

The relevant chain is:

`Evidence Creation → Provenance → AI Involvement → Authentication → Reliability → Human Accountability → Court Admission → Record Integrity`

## What the Evidence Shows

Existing evidence rules already contain mechanisms for authenticating digital material.

Under current Federal Rule of Evidence 901, a proponent must provide evidence sufficient to support a finding that an item is what the proponent claims it is. The rule gives multiple possible methods, including witness knowledge, distinctive characteristics, comparison, and evidence describing a process or system and showing that it produces an accurate result.[4]

AI has not displaced that basic architecture.

Instead, it has exposed places where the traditional architecture may not answer every question that matters.

The federal evidence rulemaking process illustrates the distinction. A possible Rule 707 has been considered for AI-produced evidence offered without an accompanying expert witness where reliability concerns resemble those addressed by Rule 702. Separately, a possible Rule 901(c) has been studied for situations in which evidence is challenged as potentially fabricated through generative AI.[1]

As of 12 September 2026, neither provision is an adopted Federal Rule of Evidence. The September 2026 report of the Committee on Rules of Practice and Procedure describes both as matters under continued consideration: a possible new Rule 707 to address evidence that is the product of artificial intelligence and a possible new subsection (c) in Rule 901 to address deepfake evidence.[5]

That status matters.

The evidence establishes that rulemaking bodies are actively examining the problem. It does not establish that U.S. courts have adopted one uniform AI-evidence standard, much less that courts across different jurisdictions follow the same approach.

## AI-Generated Evidence Is Not One Evidence Type

The phrase "AI-generated evidence" can conceal materially different processes.

An item might be:

| Evidence Process | What AI Is Doing |
| --- | --- |
| AI-assisted enhancement | Modifying or clarifying existing captured material |
| AI reconstruction | Producing a representation from underlying observations or data |
| AI analytical output | Inferring, classifying, matching, scoring, or identifying |
| Synthetic generation | Creating image, audio, video, text, or other content |
| AI-altered evidence | Changing some portion of existing material |
| Alleged synthetic evidence | Material presented as conventional evidence but challenged as AI-generated or manipulated |

These categories create different provenance and reliability questions.

An enhanced photograph begins with an underlying photograph.

A reconstruction may combine observed data with model assumptions.

A facial recognition output may be an analytical conclusion rather than a representation of what a camera directly recorded.

A fully synthetic video may have no corresponding real-world event at all.

Treating all of them simply as "digital evidence" can hide where the evidentiary transformation occurred.

## 1. Evidence Creation

The first question is no longer only who recorded, wrote, photographed, or collected the material.

It is also:

**What process produced the item now being offered?**

For conventional evidence, the path may begin with a camera, sensor, document, witness, instrument, or physical object.

With AI involvement, an additional computational layer can appear between the original source and the exhibit presented to the court.

For example:

`Physical Event → Camera → Original File → AI Enhancement → Exported Video`

or:

`Source Data → AI Model → Analytical Output → Human Review → Court Exhibit`

or:

`Prompt + Reference Material → Generative Model → Synthetic Output`

Each path produces a different evidentiary object.

The original source and the final exhibit should therefore not be assumed to carry identical evidentiary meaning.

## 2. Provenance Becomes More Granular

Traditional provenance asks where evidence came from and what happened to it after collection.

AI adds additional questions.

Relevant provenance information may include:

- The original source material.
- The system or model that processed it.
- The software or model version, where material.
- What function the AI performed.
- Which inputs were provided.
- Whether the system generated new information or transformed existing information.
- What human selections or interventions occurred.
- Whether the output was edited after generation.
- Whether the original and intermediate versions were preserved.

Not every case will require every element.

But without sufficient provenance, researchers and courts may be unable to distinguish what originated in the world from what originated in a computational process.

That distinction is central to evidentiary interpretation.

## 3. AI Involvement Must Be Described Functionally

Simply identifying that "AI was used" does not establish what AI contributed.

Consider a video.

AI might have stabilized camera motion without materially changing the depicted objects.

It might have interpolated missing frames.

It might have sharpened an unclear object by generating probable visual detail.

It might have changed a face.

It might have generated the entire scene.

Those processes should not be treated as evidentially equivalent.

The important question is therefore not merely:

**Was AI involved?**

It is:

**What part of the evidentiary representation came from the underlying source, and what part came from the AI process?**

This functional description becomes part of provenance.

## 4. Authentication Does Not Answer Every Reliability Question

Authentication asks whether an item is what its proponent claims it to be.

That formulation becomes especially important for AI-generated material.

Suppose a party offers a computer-generated reconstruction and accurately describes it as a reconstruction produced by a particular system from specified source data.

Authenticating that object as the reconstruction produced by that process does not automatically establish that the reconstruction accurately depicts the underlying event.

Similarly, proving that a file came from a particular AI system does not establish that its inference is correct.

And identifying AI involvement does not, by itself, establish that the evidence is false.

The evidentiary questions remain distinct:

`Authenticity ≠ Accuracy ≠ Reliability ≠ Truth`

That distinction is the subject of the separate Groundline Brief and should not be collapsed into a single admissibility judgment here.

## 5. Reliability Moves Toward the Process

AI-generated analytical evidence can create a different problem from a forged image.

The federal Advisory Committee has specifically examined AI output that performs work analogous to expert analysis. Its concern is that unreliable reasoning may be embedded inside a system that cannot itself be cross-examined. The Committee's work on possible Rule 707 reflects the question of how Rule 702-like reliability requirements should apply to AI-produced evidence offered without an accompanying expert witness.[1]

This changes the object of scrutiny.

The relevant question may not be whether a digital file has been altered after creation.

It may instead be whether the system that created the conclusion was sufficiently reliable for the task.

That can require attention to matters such as:

- What the system was designed to do.
- Whether it was used for that intended purpose.
- What validation supports the method.
- What error characteristics are known.
- Whether the inputs were appropriate.
- Whether the output can be independently checked.
- Whether human interpretation materially affected the result.

A file can therefore be authentic as a machine output while the machine output remains unreliable as evidence of the proposition for which it is offered.

## 6. Human Accountability Does Not Disappear

AI complicates the witness structure because a model cannot testify about its own reasoning in the ordinary human sense.

But that does not eliminate human accountability.

People remain responsible for different parts of the evidence chain.

Someone may have collected the underlying data.

Someone selected the tool.

Someone configured or prompted it.

Someone decided which inputs to use.

Someone reviewed the output.

Someone determined that the resulting material should be offered to the court.

The evidence architecture can therefore shift from asking only:

**Who witnessed this?**

toward asking:

**Who can account for each material stage by which this item became evidence?**

That is a provenance question as much as a legal one.

## 7. Deepfakes Create a Different Authentication Problem

Synthetic or allegedly synthetic evidence produces a different problem.

Instead of disclosed AI output whose reliability must be evaluated, a court may face evidence presented as an ordinary photograph, recording, video, document, or other item while another party argues that it has been fabricated.

The federal Advisory Committee has studied a possible Rule 901(c) for this situation. The working draft discussed in 2026 contemplated a two-stage process. A party challenging an item would first need to present evidence sufficient to support a finding that it had been fabricated, in whole or in part, by generative AI. If that showing were made, the proponent would then need to demonstrate to the court that the item was more likely than not authentic.[1]

The Committee concluded in May 2026 that, at least at that time, an amendment to Rule 901 addressing deepfakes was not warranted. It continued developing possible rule language while keeping the issue under study.[1]

The September 2026 Standing Committee report likewise described Rule 901(c) only as a possible new subsection under continued consideration.[5]

This illustrates a broader structural problem.

Surface plausibility may no longer provide the same degree of reassurance when synthetic manipulation is possible.

A realistic voice may be synthetic.

A familiar face may be generated or altered.

A correct background may surround an event that did not occur as represented.

Metadata may be relevant without being conclusive.

A witness may recognize people or locations even though another part of the representation has been altered.

The provenance chain therefore becomes more important when surface plausibility alone cannot resolve authenticity.

## A Court Case Shows the Provenance Problem

The New York Court of Appeals confronted this changing environment in *Matter of M.S. (M.H.)*, decided on 17 February 2026.[6]

The case did not establish that the disputed videos were AI-generated or fabricated. That distinction is important.

The court instead held that the foundation offered for the videos was insufficient and that Family Court had erred in admitting them. The majority examined the unusual origin of the videos, gaps and delays in their custody, the absence of testimony from important people in the creation and acquisition chain, and the limits of testimony that parts of the depicted environment matched the physical location.[6]

The majority also observed that the increasing prevalence of deepfake technology makes reliance on matching circumstantial details more problematic because fabricated media can preserve real people, places, or environmental features while altering material parts of a representation.[6]

The case therefore demonstrates an authentication and provenance problem without establishing that AI fabrication actually occurred.

The question was not simply whether the video looked plausible.

It was whether the foundation was sufficient to establish the evidence as what its proponent claimed it to be.

## 8. Admission Is a Gate, Not a Certificate of Truth

Evidence entering a legal proceeding may encounter multiple distinct inquiries.

Depending on the jurisdiction and the type and purpose of the evidence, these can include relevance, authentication, expert reliability, hearsay, prejudice, chain of custody, and other requirements.

Admission does not collapse those questions into a finding that an item is "true."

An admitted AI-generated reconstruction may still be challenged.

An authenticated machine output may still be assigned little weight.

An accurately identified AI process may still produce an unreliable conclusion.

Likewise, the exclusion of a particular AI-generated item does not establish that all evidence produced with the same broad technology is inadmissible.

This is another reason a universal category called "AI evidence" is analytically weak.

The evidentiary function and the specific transformation matter.

## 9. Record Integrity Extends Beyond Admission

Once material enters the legal record, another provenance problem begins.

Future readers may encounter an exhibit separated from the hearing in which its limitations were explained.

An AI-generated visualization may later circulate as though it were an original recording.

An enhanced image may lose its connection to the unmodified source.

A reconstruction may be reproduced without the assumptions used to create it.

This makes record integrity partly a preservation problem.

A durable evidence architecture benefits from preserving distinctions among:

`Original Source → Processed Version → AI Output → Presented Exhibit`

Where possible and legally appropriate, the record should make it possible to determine which object is which and what relationship exists between them.

Otherwise, evidence can gain apparent certainty as it moves farther from the context in which its limitations were originally disclosed.

## Why It Matters

AI does not eliminate the traditional problems of evidence.

Fabrication existed before generative AI.

Photographs could be manipulated.

Recordings could be edited.

Expert methodologies could fail.

Computer systems could produce erroneous results.

The structural change is one of scale, accessibility, opacity, and evidentiary distance.

AI can create plausible material with little connection to direct observation. It can also transform authentic source data through processes that are difficult for ordinary observers to evaluate.

That means visual plausibility, apparent documentary form, or machine generation cannot substitute for provenance.

The legal evidence system increasingly has to separate at least seven questions:

`What was created?`

`From what source?`

`What did AI do?`

`Can the item be authenticated?`

`Is the process reliable for this use?`

`Who can account for the transformation?`

`What exactly does admission establish?`

The result is not that AI-generated evidence should automatically be trusted or rejected.

It is that evidence architecture becomes more explicit.

## Limits and Uncertainty

This article does not attempt to state a universal legal standard for AI-generated evidence.

Evidence law differs across jurisdictions, procedural settings, evidence types, and uses. State evidence rules and case law may differ from the Federal Rules of Evidence.

The federal rulemaking materials discussed here establish active institutional consideration of AI-produced and potentially synthetic evidence. They do not establish that possible Rule 707 or possible Rule 901(c) is current federal law. The September 2026 Standing Committee report continues to describe both as possible provisions under consideration.[5]

The New York decision discussed here should also not be read as a finding that the disputed videos were deepfakes or AI-generated. The Court of Appeals resolved the case on authentication and foundational grounds.[6]

Nor does the existence of AI involvement determine evidentiary value by itself.

AI-generated, AI-assisted, AI-altered, and allegedly synthetic material must be evaluated according to what was created, how it was created, what it is offered to prove, and what evidence exists about the process.

The central change is therefore not that AI has created an entirely separate universe of evidence.

It is that provenance can no longer stop at the digital file.

When AI stands between source and record, the evidentiary chain must also account for the transformation.
