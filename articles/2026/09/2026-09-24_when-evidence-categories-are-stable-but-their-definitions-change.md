# When Evidence Categories Are Stable but Their Definitions Change

**Date:** 24 September 2026

**Domain:** Classification Definitions and Evidence Comparability

**Publication Type:** Article

## Context

Evidence categories often appear more stable than the systems that create them.

A statistical table may contain the category:

`Employed`

for many consecutive years.

An industrial dataset may continue to report:

`Manufacturing`

after an economic classification has been revised.

A health surveillance system may continue using the same disease name while changing the criteria required for a case to enter that category.

A company may report:

`Active Customer`

every quarter while changing the activity threshold used to determine who qualifies.

From the surface, the evidence appears continuous.

The label is unchanged.

The rows align.

The chart can be extended.

But a category is not defined by its label alone.

Classification rules determine what observations enter a category, what remains outside, and how borderline cases are treated.

Eurostat's guidance for NACE Rev. 2.1 illustrates this directly. The classification structure alone is not considered sufficiently self-explanatory for assigning economic activities. Eurostat therefore provides explanatory notes specifying what a category includes, what it additionally includes, and what it excludes.[3]

The central distinction is:

`Stable Category Name ≠ Stable Category Meaning`

The research question is:

**How can changes in category definitions alter the meaning of evidence even when category labels remain unchanged?**

## What the Evidence Shows

Official statistical systems treat definitions and classifications as part of the information required to interpret evidence.

Eurostat's statistical metadata system distinguishes structural information such as classifications and code lists from reference metadata describing statistical concepts, methodologies, and quality.[2]

Economic classifications provide a particularly clear example.

The United Nations International Standard Industrial Classification of All Economic Activities, or ISIC, organizes economic activity using agreed concepts, definitions, principles, and classification rules. Revision 5 was developed to better reflect the current structure of the world economy, including new industries and activities that emerged after earlier revisions.[5]

The European Union's corresponding NACE classification has also been updated.

NACE Rev. 2.1 introduces new concepts and restructures a number of existing headings. Eurostat states that the new version is being introduced into European statistics according to a defined rollout, with historical continuity supported through correspondence tables, backcasting, and, in some statistical domains, parallel reporting under the old and new classifications.[3][4]

The statistical categories continue to organize economic activity.

But what sits inside those categories can change.

That creates an evidence problem whenever continuity of the label is interpreted as continuity of the underlying measurement.

## 1. A Category Is More Than a Name

Consider a category called:

`Active Business`

The label does not tell us how a business qualifies.

One system might define it as:

`Business with at least one transaction during the previous 30 days`

Another might define it as:

`Business with revenue during the previous 12 months`

A later revision might include businesses with either revenue or payroll activity during a specified period.

All three systems could publish a category called:

`Active Business`

The visible name remains the same.

The membership rule does not.

The category therefore has at least two different properties:

`Label → What the category is called`

`Definition → What qualifies for membership`

The first is usually visible in a table.

The second determines what the evidence actually contains.

## 2. Inclusion and Exclusion Rules Shape Category Membership

Every category contains boundaries.

Those boundaries may depend on:

* Age.
* Activity.
* Revenue.
* Duration.
* Location.
* Legal status.
* Employment relationship.
* Technology.
* Ownership.
* Registration.
* Diagnostic condition.
* Measurement threshold.

Changing one of those criteria can change category membership without changing the category name.

Suppose "small business" initially means:

`Fewer than 50 employees`

and is later redefined as:

`Fewer than 100 employees`

The category remains:

`Small Business`

But firms with 50 to 99 employees can now enter it.

An increase in the number of small businesses after the revision may therefore contain both:

`Real-world Change`

and:

`Reclassification`

Exclusion rules matter in the same way.

Eurostat's NACE explanatory notes identify not only activities that belong inside each category but also borderline activities that are excluded and, where relevant, the categories into which those activities should instead be classified.[3]

Understanding a category therefore requires knowing both what enters and what stays outside.

## 3. The Same Observation Can Change Category Without Changing in Reality

A classification revision can move an observation even when the underlying entity remains unchanged.

Suppose a company performs the same economic activities in two consecutive years.

Under Classification Version 1:

`Firm A → Category X`

Under Classification Version 2:

`Firm A → Category Y`

Nothing necessarily changed inside the company.

The classification system changed around it.

A dataset may consequently show:

`Category X decreases`

and:

`Category Y increases`

without an equivalent movement in the underlying economy.

This creates an important distinction:

`Observed Entity Change`

versus:

`Classification Assignment Change`

Without classification metadata, those two forms of change can be confused.

## 4. Employment Statistics Show How a Familiar Category Can Change Meaning

International labour statistics provide a clear example.

The 19th International Conference of Labour Statisticians adopted revised standards in 2013 that define employment around work performed for pay or profit.[6]

Under the revised framework, several forms of productive activity that had previously been included within employment under earlier standards are measured separately, including own-use production work, volunteer work, and unpaid trainee work.[6]

The label:

`Employment`

did not disappear.

But the statistical boundary around employment changed.

ILO guidance explains that these changes can affect the number of people classified as employed and can also affect unemployment and other labour-underutilization measures because labour-force classifications are interconnected.[6][7]

A historical series labelled "employment" therefore cannot be assumed to represent one unchanged statistical population merely because the word remains the same.

## 5. Threshold Changes Can Alter Membership Immediately

Some classifications depend on numerical thresholds.

Examples include:

`Firm size below X employees`

`Income below Y`

`Risk score above Z`

`Account inactive for more than N days`

If the threshold changes, category membership can change even if the underlying observations do not.

Suppose:

`High Risk = Score ≥ 80`

is revised to:

`High Risk = Score ≥ 70`

Cases scoring from 70 to 79 now enter the category.

An increase in the number of high-risk cases can therefore occur without any increase in the underlying scores.

The reported category changed because its boundary changed.

Threshold information is therefore part of the evidentiary meaning of the category.

## 6. Classification Logic Can Change Without a New Threshold

Not every classification rule is numerical.

Suppose an organization's industry is initially assigned according to:

`Primary activity`

A later system might assign industry according to:

`Largest source of revenue`

or another defined criterion.

A multi-activity organization can then move between categories even if its underlying operations remain materially unchanged.

The observed source data may be the same.

The classification outcome changes because the rule applied to those observations changed.

This is why classification version matters in addition to the values themselves.

## 7. Definitions Can Change Because Reality Changes

Classification revisions are not necessarily defects.

They can be required because the world no longer fits older categories well.

The United Nations states that ISIC Revision 5 was developed to better represent the current structure of the global economy and to recognize new industries and activities that emerged after earlier revisions.[5]

Eurostat similarly states that NACE Rev. 2.1 introduces new concepts and restructures existing headings while preserving older structures where change is not necessary.[3]

Classification systems therefore face two legitimate objectives:

`Current Relevance`

and:

`Historical Comparability`

A classification that never changes may gradually become less capable of describing the world.

A classification that changes can make historical comparison more difficult.

The evidence problem is not that definitions should never change.

It is that definitional change needs to remain visible.

## 8. Classification Versions Matter Alongside Codes

Classification codes often appear more precise than category labels.

They are valuable identifiers.

But a code belongs to a particular classification system and version.

The relevant evidence identity is therefore closer to:

`Classification + Version + Code`

than:

`Code Alone`

Eurostat publishes correspondence tables between NACE Rev. 2 and NACE Rev. 2.1 because economic activities can move between categories when the classification is revised.[4]

The United Nations similarly maintains previous ISIC versions and correspondence information for comparing classifications over time.[5]

A code without its governing version can therefore be insufficient for historical interpretation.

## 9. Classification Revisions Can Split Categories

A historical category may be divided when a classification becomes more detailed.

For example:

`Old Category A`

may become:

`New Category A1`

`New Category A2`

`New Category A3`

The historical series contains all three activities together.

The new series separates them.

A researcher cannot automatically construct a long historical series for A1 if the older data never distinguished the information required to identify A1.

The problem is not a missing cell.

The older classification recorded less detail.

Historical microdata or other supporting information may be required before earlier observations can be reconstructed under the newer system.

## 10. Categories Can Also Merge

The reverse can happen.

Several historical categories may become one:

`Old A + Old B + Old C → New D`

In a simple case, historical observations can potentially be aggregated to approximate the new category.

But that works only if the definition of New D corresponds adequately to the union of the older categories.

If some activities move elsewhere or new inclusion rules apply, simple addition may not reproduce the new definition.

A similar problem occurs in many-to-many transitions, where observations from several old categories are redistributed across several new ones.

These transitions can require additional information, estimation, bridge coding, backcasting, or an explicit break in historical continuity.

## 11. Correspondence Tables Preserve the Relationship Between Versions

Eurostat describes correspondence tables as important tools for comparing statistical data collected and presented using different classifications.[4]

Tables between different versions of the same classification document detailed changes introduced by a revision.

They can show how an old category relates to one or more categories in the new system.

This makes correspondence information important evidence for historical comparison.

The table does not automatically make two classification versions equivalent.

It makes their relationship visible.

That distinction matters because some mappings are simple while others are not.

## 12. Backcasting Can Improve Historical Comparability

One response to classification change is to reconstruct historical statistics using the new classification.

Eurostat describes backcasting as a way to provide historical time series based on the same version of NACE and has developed plans and guidance for producing historical NACE Rev. 2.1 series.[4]

Conceptually:

`Historical Evidence Under Old Classification`

`→ Reclassification or Backcasting`

`→ Historical Series Under New Classification`

This can substantially improve comparability.

But the quality of the reconstructed series depends on the historical information available.

If the newer classification requires information that was never recorded under the older system, reconstruction may require assumptions, models, auxiliary information, or proportional allocation.

A backcasted value can therefore be useful without being identical in evidentiary status to an observation originally classified under the new system.

## 13. Parallel Reporting Can Make Classification Change Observable

Another transition strategy is to produce statistics under both old and new classifications for an overlap period.

Eurostat's NACE transition includes double reporting for some statistical domains.[4]

That can create bridge evidence:

`Same Underlying Period → Old Classification`

and:

`Same Underlying Period → New Classification`

The difference between the resulting statistics provides information about the effect of the classification change itself.

Parallel classification therefore helps distinguish changes created by the measurement system from changes in the underlying economy.

## 14. A Break in Series Is Evidence

When a classification or methodology changes substantially, a statistical series may require a documented break.

Eurostat's quality guidance treats comparability over time as a separate quality dimension and recognizes that methodological or definitional change may affect continuity.[1]

A break in a series should therefore not be understood merely as a formatting inconvenience.

It communicates something about the evidence:

**Observations on opposite sides of the break were not produced under sufficiently equivalent conditions to be treated as one uninterrupted series without qualification.**

Removing the visible break from a chart does not restore comparability.

The underlying evidence conditions remain different.

## 15. Classification Change Can Look Like Real-world Change

Suppose a category contains:

`100 units in Year 1`

and:

`130 units in Year 2`

The visible change is:

`+30`

If the category definition changed between those observations, the difference may reflect more than one influence.

Conceptually:

`Observed Change`

may contain:

`Real-world Change`

plus:

`Reclassification Effect`

plus other measurement differences.

This is not a universal statistical equation.

It illustrates why a numerical trend cannot always be interpreted as pure real-world movement when the classification changed.

## 16. Stable Totals Can Also Hide Reclassification

Classification change does not always produce a visible jump.

Suppose:

`Category X, Year 1 = 500`

`Category X, Year 2 = 500`

The total appears perfectly stable.

But some former members may have been reclassified out while newly eligible observations were reclassified in.

The total did not change.

The composition did.

Historical comparability therefore concerns more than whether aggregate values display an obvious discontinuity.

It also concerns whether the category represents sufficiently similar observations across periods.

## 17. Category Definitions Can Affect Derived Indicators

A changed category can propagate into downstream measures.

The ILO labour-statistics example demonstrates this clearly.

Changing the definition of employment can change:

`Number Employed`

and can also affect:

`Labour Force`

and therefore indicators derived from those quantities.[6][7]

The same problem occurs elsewhere.

Suppose:

`Failure Rate = Failed Units / Total Units`

If the definition of a failed unit changes, the rate can change.

If the definition of the denominator changes as well, interpretation becomes still more difficult.

A derived metric can retain the same name while inheriting definitional changes from its components.

The formula alone therefore does not guarantee historical continuity.

## 18. The Same Problem Appears Across Institutions

Definitional instability is not only a time-series issue.

Different organizations can use the same label for different categories.

Two agencies may report:

`Small Enterprise`

using different employee or revenue thresholds.

Two hospital systems may report:

`Readmission`

using different time windows.

Two digital businesses may report:

`Active User`

using different activity criteria.

The shared label can make the measurements look standardized when their operational definitions are not.

This is why category comparability matters both:

`Across Time`

and:

`Across Systems`

Before broader contextual differences are considered, researchers first need to know whether the category itself represents the same thing.

## 19. Structural Continuity Does Not Guarantee Conceptual Continuity

Eurostat distinguishes structural metadata from reference metadata.[2]

Structural metadata identify elements such as dimensions, classifications, codes, and units.

Reference metadata describe the concepts, methodologies, quality characteristics, and other information required to interpret the observations.

A dataset may therefore preserve the same category column and even the same familiar label while the conceptual definition behind that category changes.

This creates another useful distinction:

`Visible Category ≠ Full Category Meaning`

Structural continuity can survive while conceptual continuity does not.

## 20. Classification Systems Are Part of Measurement

Categories are often treated as passive containers for evidence.

They are not.

Classification systems determine how complex observations become discrete groups.

Economic activity becomes industries.

Labour status becomes employment, unemployment, or outside the labour force.

Organizations become small, medium, or large.

Events become particular incident types.

Transactions become categories of expenditure.

Every classification creates boundaries.

Those boundaries affect what researchers later count, compare, aggregate, and interpret.

Classification design is therefore part of the measurement system that produces evidence.

## 21. Definitional Change Can Improve Evidence

A loss of perfect historical continuity does not necessarily mean evidence quality has declined.

The ILO revised labour standards to provide clearer distinctions among different forms of work and improve labour-market measurement.[6]

ISIC Rev. 5 and NACE Rev. 2.1 similarly update economic classifications to reflect newer activities and economic structures more effectively.[3][5]

The relevant objective is therefore not permanent definitional immobility.

A more useful principle is:

`Improved Definition + Visible Transition`

A revised classification can improve evidence about the present while still requiring caution when linking that evidence to the past.

## 22. Renaming Categories Does Not Harmonize Them

A tempting response to definitional differences is to give several categories one common name.

For example:

`Definition A → Employment`

`Definition B → Employment`

and then combine them.

The labels now match.

The evidence does not necessarily do so.

Real harmonization requires evidence that the membership rules can be reconciled sufficiently for the intended use.

If they cannot, maintaining separate categories may preserve the evidence more accurately than forcing an artificial continuous series.

Semantic similarity alone is not enough.

Two definitions can sound nearly identical while producing different category membership.

Operational meaning matters more than linguistic resemblance.

## 23. Comparability Remains Claim-specific

Definitions do not have to be perfectly identical for every form of comparison.

Suppose two versions of a manufacturing classification differ only in one relatively small activity.

The historical series may remain adequate for a broad macroeconomic question.

The same difference may become decisive for research focused specifically on that affected industry.

The relevant question is therefore:

**Are the category definitions sufficiently compatible for the particular claim being made?**

not:

**Are the classifications identical in every respect?**

This keeps the evidentiary requirement proportional to the research question.

## Why It Matters

Categories are among the least visible components of evidence systems.

Researchers usually notice values first.

The category can appear to be only the container holding those values.

But the category determines which observations are allowed into that container.

When the rule changes, the evidence can change.

If the definition expands, new observations can enter.

If it narrows, previous members can leave.

If classification rules change, an unchanged entity can move between categories.

If a threshold changes, category membership can shift immediately.

If new economic activities are recognized, a classification can become more relevant while historical continuity becomes more complicated.

This creates a recurring risk whenever a researcher sees:

`Same Category`

across several years and interprets it as:

`Same Measurement Meaning`

A stable category name establishes continuity of naming.

It does not independently establish continuity of evidence.

## Limits and Uncertainty

This article does not argue that every definitional revision destroys comparability.

Some changes may have little material effect on category membership.

Some classification versions may correspond closely.

Other revisions may require correspondence tables, historical reconstruction, or explicit breaks in series.

Eurostat's materials cited here concern official statistical classifications and statistical-quality reporting.[1][3][4]

NACE and ISIC specifically classify economic activities.[3][5]

The ILO example concerns international labour-statistics standards.[6][7]

Their specific transition practices should not be transferred mechanically to every research domain.

They nevertheless support a narrower general principle:

**A category label cannot establish category continuity by itself.**

The public sources reviewed do not establish that:

* Every wording change alters category membership.
* Every classification revision produces a material break.
* Newer classifications are inherently superior for every analytical purpose.
* Historical observations can always be reconstructed under newer definitions.
* Correspondence tables eliminate all classification uncertainty.
* Backcasted values are always equivalent to directly classified observations.
* Identical labels or codes establish identical category composition.
* A stable aggregate value establishes stable underlying membership.

Historical evidence can remain usable after a classification change.

It may be sufficiently comparable without adjustment.

It may be reconciled.

It may be backcast.

It may require a documented break.

Or the remaining difference may be immaterial to the particular claim.

Those conclusions require information about the classification system itself.

`Stable Category Name ≠ Stable Category Meaning`

A label tells researchers what a category is called.

Its definition determines what evidence the category actually contains.
