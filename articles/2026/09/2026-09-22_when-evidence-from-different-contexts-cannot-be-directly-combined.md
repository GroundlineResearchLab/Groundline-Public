# When Evidence From Different Contexts Cannot Be Directly Combined

**Date:** 22 September 2026  

**Domain:** Cross-Context Evidence Comparability  

**Publication Type:** Article

## Context

Evidence can be valid in one context and misleading when transferred uncritically into another.

A national employment statistic may accurately describe one country's labour market.

A hospital dataset may accurately describe patients treated within one health system.

A corporate operating metric may accurately describe one company's facilities.

A survey may be methodologically sound for the population from which its respondents were sampled.

None of those properties automatically establishes that the resulting evidence can be directly compared with evidence produced elsewhere.

The problem becomes especially important when researchers combine datasets because they appear to measure the same thing.

Two datasets may both contain:

`Employment Rate`

`Energy Consumption`

`Hospital Admission Rate`

`Average Income`

`Productivity`

`Capacity`

`Failure Rate`

`Water Use`

Yet the evidence underlying those labels may have been produced using different populations, geographic boundaries, institutional systems, definitions, reference periods, sampling frames, operating conditions, or measurement procedures.

Eurostat treats comparability as a distinct statistical quality issue and identifies differences in concepts, definitions, measurement tools, and procedures as potential sources of non-comparability.[1]

The World Bank similarly warns that statistical methods, coverage, practices, and definitions differ across countries and that full comparability cannot always be assured even after substantial standardization work.[3]

The relevant research question is therefore not simply:

**Is each dataset valid?**

It is:

**Are the contexts in which those datasets were produced sufficiently compatible for the comparison or combination being attempted?**

The central distinction is:

`Valid Evidence in Context A + Valid Evidence in Context B ≠ Directly Comparable Evidence`

## What the Evidence Shows

Evidence synthesis systems already treat context as part of the evidence rather than as information that can simply be discarded.

Cochrane requires systematic reviews to define relevant populations, interventions, comparators, outcomes, and study designs and to determine how different studies will be grouped for synthesis.[4]

Its data collection guidance also emphasizes participant characteristics, setting, recruitment, sampling, study timing, intervention conditions, and other contextual information that may affect interpretation or applicability.[5]

Before statistical combination, Cochrane warns that variation among populations, interventions, outcomes, study designs, and outcome measurement can create important forms of heterogeneity. Meta-analysis can be seriously misleading when those differences are not considered carefully.[6]

Eurostat approaches the same problem through statistical metadata. Its metadata systems preserve information about statistical concepts, classifications, time dimensions, methodologies, quality, and other characteristics required to interpret statistical values.[2]

These systems come from different fields.

They support the same broader principle:

**Context is part of the meaning of evidence.**

A number does not become context-free simply because it can be copied into another table.

## 1. Evidence Has a Context Boundary

Every measurement is produced inside a boundary.

That boundary may determine:

- Who was observed.
- What was measured.
- Where measurement occurred.
- When measurement occurred.
- What unit was used.
- Which cases were included.
- Which cases were excluded.
- Which classifications applied.
- Which institutions governed the activity.
- Which methodology produced the result.
- Which operating conditions prevailed.

A numerical value therefore carries more meaning than the number alone.

Eurostat's distinction between structural and reference metadata illustrates this directly. Structural metadata identify dimensions and classifications needed to understand a value, while reference metadata describe concepts, methodologies, quality, and other information needed for interpretation.[2]

A value separated from those characteristics can remain numerically intact while becoming evidentially ambiguous.

Cross-context comparison is therefore not only a mathematical problem.

It is also a problem of defining what each measurement actually represents.

## 2. Population Differences Can Change the Meaning of the Same Result

Suppose two surveys report an employment rate of 70 percent.

At first sight:

`Country A = 70%`

`Country B = 70%`

The numbers appear directly comparable.

But the underlying populations may differ.

One measure might cover people aged 15 to 64.

Another might use a different age range.

One may be based on the usually resident population.

Another may depend on registered residents.

One may exclude particular institutional populations.

Another may include them.

The reported percentage can therefore be identical while the denominator represents a different population.

Eurostat population metadata illustrate why this distinction matters. Population statistics can depend on concepts such as usual residence, legal residence, or registered residence, depending on the statistical system and applicable definitions.[2]

The evidence question is therefore not merely:

**Are the percentages expressed in the same format?**

It is:

**Do they refer to sufficiently comparable populations?**

## 3. Geographic Context Is More Than Location

Two measurements made in different places may differ for reasons that are not visible in the reported value.

Relevant geographic conditions can include:

- Climate.
- Population density.
- Urbanization.
- Terrain.
- Infrastructure.
- Resource availability.
- Market structure.
- Environmental exposure.
- Distance and accessibility.

Consider two industrial facilities reporting the same energy intensity.

One may operate in a cool environment.

Another may require substantial cooling.

One may perform final assembly.

Another may perform energy-intensive upstream processing within the same facility boundary.

Both values could be accurately measured.

Their equality does not necessarily establish equivalent operational efficiency.

The measurement context matters because geography can alter the conditions under which the metric is produced.

## 4. Institutional Context Can Change What Is Being Recorded

Evidence is also shaped by institutions.

Administrative systems define categories through rules.

An unemployment statistic may depend on labour-force definitions, eligibility rules, registration systems, or reporting practices.

A business-failure statistic may depend on insolvency law and court procedures.

A health statistic may depend on insurance structures, diagnostic coding, access to hospitals, or referral systems.

Two jurisdictions may therefore record the same numerical count while counting somewhat different underlying conditions.

For example:

`Jurisdiction A: 1,000 insolvencies`

`Jurisdiction B: 1,000 insolvencies`

The counts can both be correct.

But if the legal definition of an insolvency proceeding differs, the figures do not necessarily represent equivalent economic events.

Institutional context can become part of the measurement itself.

## 5. Regulatory Boundaries Can Change the Observable Population

Regulation can determine which entities or events enter a dataset.

One jurisdiction may require facilities above a particular emissions threshold to report.

Another may use a different threshold.

Another may exempt particular industries.

All three datasets may accurately represent their respective reporting regimes.

But a comparison of the number of reporting facilities can be misleading if reporting boundaries differ.

A higher recorded count might reflect:

- A lower reporting threshold.
- Wider industry coverage.
- Different exemptions.
- Different registration rules.
- Different enforcement conditions.

The observed dataset therefore partly reflects the regulatory system that produced it.

A reporting difference should not automatically be interpreted as a real-world difference in the underlying phenomenon.

## 6. Operating Conditions Can Change Performance Metrics

Operational evidence is often sensitive to the conditions under which performance is measured.

Two data centers may use the same efficiency metric.

One may operate near full load.

Another may operate far below design utilization.

One may experience high ambient temperatures.

Another may operate in a cooler environment.

Even where the metric definition is identical, operating conditions can affect the resulting value.

The same issue can appear in:

- Manufacturing yield.
- Agricultural productivity.
- Vehicle fuel consumption.
- Network throughput.
- Equipment failure rates.
- Water use.
- Energy intensity.
- Hospital utilization.

The measurements may be accurate.

The problem arises when contextual conditions that affect those measurements are treated as though they were equivalent.

`Same Metric + Different Conditions ≠ Automatically Equivalent Performance`

## 7. Definitions Must Be Aligned Before Values Are Combined

Some differences remain hidden inside ordinary words.

Consider:

`Capacity`

Depending on the source, capacity might refer to:

- Installed capacity.
- Nameplate capacity.
- Available capacity.
- Tested maximum capacity.
- Contracted capacity.
- Average operating capacity.
- Usable capacity under specified conditions.

Two datasets may each use the word correctly according to their own documentation.

If their definitions differ, merging them into a single "capacity" column can create apparent uniformity that did not exist in the source evidence.

The same problem can occur with terms such as:

`Productivity`

`Efficiency`

`Revenue`

`Incident`

`Employment`

`Failure`

`Availability`

The common label should therefore be treated as the beginning of the comparison, not proof that the underlying measurement is the same.

## 8. Sampling Frames Can Produce Different Evidence Universes

Two surveys can ask identical questions and still represent different populations.

Suppose:

`Survey A samples registered firms.`

`Survey B samples tax-paying firms.`

`Survey C samples firms listed in a commercial database.`

Each survey may correctly sample from its own frame.

But those frames may contain different businesses.

One may exclude informal enterprises.

Another may exclude recently established firms.

Another may disproportionately represent larger businesses.

Combining the observations can create a larger dataset without creating one common evidence universe.

Cochrane's requirement to define relevant study populations before synthesis reflects the same general principle: evidence belongs first to the population from which it was produced.[4][5]

## 9. Time Can Make Contexts Different Even When Methods Match

Two datasets may use the same methodology but describe different periods.

Suppose one country's productivity data are from 2025 while another country's are from 2020.

The measurement method may be identical.

The surrounding system may not be.

Between those years, conditions may have changed through:

- Technology adoption.
- Inflation.
- Regulation.
- Energy costs.
- Demography.
- Trade conditions.
- Infrastructure.
- Economic cycles.
- Exceptional disruptions.

The World Bank notes that timing, data availability, survey age, and reporting practices can affect international indicators and their interpretation.[3]

Temporal alignment is therefore part of cross-context comparability.

Matching metric names and units does not make different periods equivalent.

## 10. Measurement Boundaries Can Produce Different Quantities

One of the most consequential comparison problems occurs when system boundaries differ.

Consider two companies reporting emissions.

Company A reports:

`Direct operational emissions`

Company B reports:

`Direct operational emissions + purchased electricity emissions`

Both totals could be correct according to their respective reporting boundaries.

They should not be compared as though they represent identical quantities.

Similar problems can occur with:

**Revenue**

Gross versus net reporting.

**Water**

Withdrawals versus consumption.

**Energy**

Site energy versus a wider energy boundary.

**Production**

Gross production versus saleable production.

**Infrastructure**

Operational assets versus operational plus under-construction assets.

Context therefore includes what is inside and outside the measurement boundary.

A correct value inside one boundary can become misleading when compared with a value generated from another.

## 11. Common Units Do Not Solve Conceptual Differences

Researchers can often convert values into common units.

Kilograms can become tonnes.

Currencies can be converted.

Energy units can be standardized.

Counts can become rates.

These operations solve mathematical incompatibilities.

They do not necessarily solve conceptual ones.

Suppose two productivity measures are converted into:

`USD per worker`

The values may still differ in their treatment of:

- Worker definitions.
- Work hours.
- Informal labour.
- Industry composition.
- Price levels.
- Exchange-rate methodology.
- Output definitions.

The calculation may now use one unit.

The underlying evidence may still measure different things.

The distinction is:

`Common Unit ≠ Common Measurement Context`

## 12. Denominators Are Part of the Evidence

Rates and ratios can appear especially comparable because they are already normalized.

Examples include:

`Cases per 100,000 people`

`Output per worker`

`Energy per unit produced`

`Revenue per customer`

But a ratio contains at least two measurement structures:

`Numerator / Denominator`

Both matter.

Two regions may report the same disease incidence per 100,000 people.

One may use broader diagnostic criteria.

The other may have different access to testing.

Their population denominators may also be defined differently.

An identical rate therefore does not guarantee an identical underlying condition.

Normalization reduces scale differences.

It does not automatically remove measurement differences.

## 13. Collection Methods Can Produce Method Effects

Evidence may differ because of how observations were generated.

Possible methods include:

- Face-to-face interviews.
- Telephone interviews.
- Online questionnaires.
- Administrative records.
- Sensors.
- Manual inspection.
- Self-reporting.
- Automated logs.
- Satellite observation.

Different collection systems may observe related phenomena differently.

Self-reported working hours may differ from electronic time records.

Survey-based expenditures may differ from transaction records.

Satellite land observations may differ from administrative land registries.

Neither source must be invalid.

The relevant question is whether differences in results represent the underlying phenomenon, differences in collection method, or some combination of the two.

## 14. Evidence About the Same Topic Can Answer Different Questions

Two datasets can concern the same general topic while measuring different propositions.

Consider:

**Dataset A**

Measures how many workers report using AI tools.

**Dataset B**

Measures how many tasks are technically exposed to potential AI automation.

Both concern AI and work.

They do not measure the same thing.

Combining them into one "AI adoption rate" would create a metric that neither dataset originally produced.

Evidence comparison therefore requires alignment not only of numbers but also of the claims those numbers can support.

Evidence about the same topic is not automatically evidence of the same proposition.

## 15. Statistical Pooling Requires More Than Valid Individual Studies

Systematic review methodology provides a clear example.

Cochrane does not treat all individually valid studies addressing a broad topic as automatically suitable for statistical combination.

Reviewers define which populations, interventions, comparators, outcomes, and study designs belong together before synthesis.[4]

Cochrane also distinguishes variability arising from participants, interventions, and outcomes from variability caused by study design or measurement methods.[6]

Its meta-analysis guidance states that statistical combination can seriously mislead if variation among studies is not considered, and that meta-analysis should only be considered when studies are sufficiently compatible to produce a meaningful summary.[6]

The general lesson extends beyond clinical research:

**More valid evidence does not automatically justify one combined number.**

Sometimes evidence should remain separate.

Sometimes it may require subgroup analysis or contextual interpretation.

Sometimes harmonisation is possible.

The presence of multiple valid sources does not itself determine which of those choices is appropriate.

## 16. Averaging Can Hide the Difference That Matters

Suppose two systems produce:

`Context A = 20`

`Context B = 80`

The arithmetic mean is:

`50`

The calculation is correct.

Its meaning depends on what A and B represent.

If they are comparable observations of the same underlying quantity, the average may be useful.

If they represent structurally different systems, the value 50 may describe neither system particularly well.

This is especially important when contexts differ systematically, such as:

- Rural and metropolitan regions.
- Small firms and multinational firms.
- Low-resource and high-resource hospitals.
- Stable and constrained infrastructure systems.
- Different regulatory regimes.

Aggregation can remove the very contextual variation that explains why the measurements differ.

Cochrane similarly warns that strong heterogeneity can make an overall average misleading, especially when the direction or size of effects differs substantially across studies.[6]

## 17. A Larger Combined Dataset Is Not Automatically Stronger Comparable Evidence

Pooling datasets usually increases the number of observations.

Suppose:

`Dataset A = 10,000 observations`

`Dataset B = 15,000 observations`

Combined:

`25,000 observations`

The new dataset is larger.

But if A and B use incompatible definitions, populations, or measurement boundaries, the larger sample does not repair those differences.

Greater observation volume can improve precision within an appropriate measurement structure.

It does not automatically make different evidence structures equivalent.

For cross-context research:

`Larger Combined N ≠ Automatically Stronger Comparable Evidence`

## 18. Harmonization Makes Cross-Context Comparison Possible

The problem does not mean that evidence from different contexts should never be compared.

International statistics exist precisely because institutions invest substantial effort in making comparison possible.

Harmonization may involve:

- Common definitions.
- Common classifications.
- Standard units.
- Aligned reference periods.
- Consistent statistical populations.
- Common questionnaires.
- Shared methodological rules.
- Metadata standards.
- Validation procedures.
- Documented adjustment methods.

Eurostat's statistical infrastructure illustrates how common concepts, classifications, metadata, and methodologies can improve comparability across countries.[1][2]

The important point is that comparability is produced through methodological alignment.

It should not be assumed simply because datasets can be joined technically.

## 19. Harmonization Does Not Eliminate Every Difference

Standardization can substantially improve comparability without making contexts identical.

The World Bank explicitly states that considerable effort is made to standardize international development data, but full comparability cannot always be assured because statistical systems differ in methods, coverage, practices, and definitions.[3]

This means that harmonized international indicators should still be interpreted with their metadata and methodological qualifications.

Standardization reduces incompatibility.

It does not make context disappear.

## 20. Metadata Preserve What a Table Can Hide

When data are downloaded into a spreadsheet, much of their meaning can disappear.

A table might retain only:

`Country`

`Year`

`Indicator`

`Value`

while the source metadata contain:

- Definition.
- Statistical population.
- Unit.
- Reference period.
- Source data.
- Methodology.
- Classification.
- Coverage.
- Quality information.
- Comparability notes.

Eurostat explicitly states that data and structural metadata must go together and that reference metadata help users interpret statistical data by documenting concepts and methodologies.[2]

This matters because a number stripped of its metadata may still look precise.

What has disappeared is the information required to determine what the number actually represents.

## 21. Generalization Is Different From Comparison

Cross-context analysis can involve at least two separate operations.

**Comparison** asks:

**How are Context A and Context B similar or different?**

**Generalization** asks:

**Can evidence from Context A support a conclusion about Context B?**

These are not the same operation.

Two countries may legitimately be compared precisely because their statistical definitions differ.

The difference itself may be the research finding.

That does not mean their numerical values can be pooled.

Likewise, a result produced in one hospital, market, region, or industrial system may be valid locally without establishing that the same relationship will hold elsewhere.

Cochrane collects information about participants and settings partly so that differences among studies and the applicability of findings to other populations can be evaluated.[5]

Moving evidence from one context to another therefore requires an additional inference.

Local validity is not automatic external validity.

## Why It Matters

Modern data systems make combination technically easy.

Datasets can be joined in seconds.

Units can be normalized automatically.

International rankings can be generated from large collections of indicators.

Dashboards can place countries, hospitals, companies, regions, factories, or technologies beside one another.

The technical ability to combine evidence can therefore arrive before the evidentiary justification for combining it.

This creates several risks.

A population difference can look like a performance difference.

A reporting difference can look like an incidence difference.

A regulatory difference can look like a behavioral difference.

A system-boundary difference can look like an efficiency difference.

A methodological difference can look like a real-world difference.

An important contextual effect can disappear inside an average.

In each case, the original evidence may still be valid.

The error occurs in the relationship constructed between the evidence.

That is why cross-context research must preserve a distinction between:

`Evidence Quality Within a Context`

and:

`Comparability Across Contexts`

Strong evidence on both sides does not automatically create a strong comparison between them.

## Limits and Uncertainty

This article does not establish that evidence from different contexts should never be combined.

Cross-country statistics, systematic reviews, comparative research, multi-site studies, benchmarking systems, and many other research environments routinely compare or combine evidence produced under different conditions.

Such comparisons can be highly informative.

The issue is whether comparability has been examined rather than assumed.

The European Statistical System materials cited here concern official statistics and metadata.[1][2]

World Bank guidance concerns international development statistics.[3]

Cochrane guidance concerns systematic reviews, primarily in health research.[4][5][6]

Their detailed procedures should not be transferred mechanically into every research domain.

They support a narrower general principle:

**Evidence that is valid in its original context is not automatically compatible with evidence generated in another context.**

The sources reviewed do not establish that:

- Different contexts must be identical before comparison is possible.
- Every contextual difference materially affects every measurement.
- Harmonized data are unreliable.
- Evidence from different populations can never be synthesized.
- Normalization and adjustment are ineffective.
- Statistical pooling is inappropriate whenever any heterogeneity exists.
- One universal comparability threshold applies to every research question.

Comparability depends partly on the claim being made.

Two datasets may be adequate for a broad descriptive comparison while being unsuitable for precise ranking, statistical pooling, causal inference, or direct generalization.

Sometimes contextual differences can be harmonized.

Sometimes they can be adjusted.

Sometimes they should remain explicit.

Sometimes the evidence should remain separate.

The central distinction remains:

`Valid Evidence in Context A + Valid Evidence in Context B ≠ Directly Comparable Evidence`

The question is not merely whether each source is credible.

It is whether the sources represent sufficiently comparable things for the conclusion being drawn.
