# Groundline-Public Explainers

**Project:** Groundline Research Lab

**Repository:** Groundline-Public

**Section:** Public Explainers

**Framework:** DGCP™

## Purpose

The `explainers/` directory contains public educational material designed to explain how data, evidence, research methods, and information systems work.

Explainers are intended to improve public understanding.

They may simplify technical concepts, but they must preserve evidence, context, definitions, limitations, and important distinctions.

## Scope

Explainers may cover topics including:

• What data is.

• How data is collected.

• How data is classified.

• How data is cleaned.

• How data is transformed.

• How provenance works.

• How sources differ.

• How evidence is evaluated.

• How datasets are constructed.

• How missing data should be interpreted.

• How uncertainty is represented.

• How research conclusions are formed.

• How verification works.

• How data quality affects interpretation.

• How data moves through systems.

• How public statistics are produced.

• How AI systems use data.

• How research records preserve traceability.

The scope may expand as Groundline Research Lab develops new educational domains.

## Explainer Function

An explainer should help a reader understand a concept, process, relationship, or research practice.

It may answer questions such as:

1. What is this concept?
2. How does it work?
3. Why does it matter?
4. What can go wrong?
5. What should a reader check?
6. What does the available evidence establish?
7. What does the evidence not establish?

Explainers should support understanding rather than persuasion.

## Evidence Standard

Educational material must remain evidence based.

An explainer should not introduce certainty merely because the subject is being simplified.

Where a concept depends on context, methodology, jurisdiction, data source, or research design, that dependency should remain visible.

Examples should not be presented as universal rules unless the evidence supports that generalization.

## Definitions

Important terms should be defined clearly.

Definitions should distinguish between concepts that may appear similar but have different meanings.

Examples include:

• Data and evidence.

• Source and provenance.

• Missing and zero.

• Correlation and causation.

• Estimate and measurement.

• Record and interpretation.

• Public access and unrestricted reuse.

• Data availability and data quality.

Definitions should remain consistent within a publication.

## Simplification

Explainers may reduce technical complexity for accessibility.

Simplification must not remove distinctions that materially affect meaning.

A simplified explanation should still preserve:

• Scope.

• Context.

• Limitations.

• Uncertainty.

• Relevant dependencies.

• Evidence status.

## Examples

Examples may be used to make concepts easier to understand.

Examples should be identified as illustrative when they are not direct research findings.

A hypothetical example should not be presented as an observed event.

A simplified example should not be treated as a complete representation of a real system.

## Sources

Where an explainer relies on external definitions, research, standards, datasets, or institutional material, those sources should be identified.

Primary or authoritative sources should be preferred where materially relevant.

Source selection should remain appropriate to the specific concept being explained.

## Explainer Structure

A typical explainer may use:

```text id="c8km2q"
# Title

## What It Is

## How It Works

## Why It Matters

## Example

## Common Misunderstandings

## Limits

## Sources
```

This structure is optional and may be adapted to the subject.

## Common Misunderstandings

Where useful, explainers may identify common interpretation errors.

Examples include:

• Treating all public data as open data.

• Assuming more data automatically means better evidence.

• Treating missing data as zero.

• Assuming an official source cannot contain limitations.

• Treating a dataset as neutral without examining its construction.

• Treating a model output as direct observation.

• Confusing data access with data rights.

• Confusing provenance with proof of correctness.

These sections should clarify concepts rather than criticize readers.

## Public Readability

Language should be clear, direct, and suitable for the intended audience.

Technical terms may be used where necessary but should be explained when they are essential to understanding.

Educational accessibility should not come at the cost of research integrity.

## Corrections

Material errors should be corrected transparently.

Where a correction changes the explanation substantially, the relationship between the original and corrected version should remain traceable.

## Publication Boundary

Only explainers approved for public release belong in this directory.

Internal teaching notes, incomplete research, unpublished methodology, private examples, and restricted material should remain outside `Groundline-Public`.
