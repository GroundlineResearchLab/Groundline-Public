# Can Aggregation Hide a Pattern That Is Present in the Underlying Observations?

**Date:** 29 September 2026  

**Domain:** Evidence Resolution and Aggregation  

**Publication Type:** Brief

## Question

Can aggregation make a pattern disappear from reported data even when that pattern remains present in the underlying observations?

## What the Evidence Shows

Yes.

Aggregation combines observations into broader geographic areas, longer periods, larger population groups, broader categories, or summary measures.

That process can make evidence easier to report, stabilize, compare, or interpret.

It can also remove distinctions that were visible at a finer level.

The central distinction is:

**No Pattern in Aggregated Data ≠ No Pattern in the Underlying Observations**

The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention provides a direct example.

CDC documentation on county-level suicide statistics explains that small numbers can produce unstable estimates. Two traditional approaches to improving stability have been to combine counties into larger geographic units or aggregate observations across multiple years.[1]

CDC notes that both approaches can mask important geographic and temporal trends occurring at the individual county level.[1]

The observations have not necessarily disappeared.

Their differences have been combined into a broader representation.

## Aggregation Changes Evidence Resolution

Different forms of aggregation can remove different kinds of detail.

| Aggregation | Detail That May Become Less Visible |
| --- | --- |
| Geographic aggregation | Differences among neighborhoods, counties, districts, or other smaller areas |
| Temporal aggregation | Short-duration peaks, declines, disruptions, or changes within the combined period |
| Population aggregation | Differences among demographic or other subgroups |
| Category aggregation | Variation among categories combined into a broader group |
| Numerical averaging | Extremes, dispersion, and differences among individual observations |

An aggregate can therefore be valid while lacking the resolution required for a narrower question.

A yearly value may correctly summarize a year while failing to show a disruption lasting several days.

A national average may correctly summarize a national population while concealing substantial regional differences.

A combined population category may correctly represent a total while hiding differences among the groups inside it.

The issue is not necessarily whether the aggregate is correct.

It is what the aggregate is capable of showing.

## Population Aggregation Can Conceal Subgroup Differences

A CDC study of COVID-19 outcomes in Hawaii provides a direct example.

The researchers examined Native Hawaiian, Pacific Islander, and Asian populations using more detailed population categories.[2]

The disaggregated analysis identified substantial differences among specific groups.

Among Pacific Islander populations, particularly high COVID-19 incidence was observed among Marshallese, other Micronesian, and Samoan persons. Among Asian populations, incidence also varied substantially, including higher rates among Filipino and Vietnamese persons than among some other Asian subgroups.[2]

The authors concluded that important disparities in COVID-19 incidence and mortality had been obscured in aggregated data.[2]

Disaggregation did not create those cases or deaths.

It changed the resolution at which the existing evidence was examined.

This creates an important distinction:

`Same Underlying Events`

can produce:

`Broad Aggregate: Limited Visible Difference`

and:

`More Detailed Evidence: Distinct Subgroup Pattern`

The difference lies partly in the resolution of the representation.

## Geographic Aggregation Can Conceal Local Variation

The same issue appears geographically.

Research associated with CDC's Environmental Public Health Tracking Program explains that state-level data can mask disparities among counties and that county-level data can likewise mask substantial variation within counties.[3]

The authors note that local socioeconomic conditions, environmental exposures, and health risks can vary considerably inside one county.[3]

A county-level statistic can therefore be accurate for the county as a whole without accurately representing every community inside it.

CDC's current Tracking Program similarly emphasizes that finer geographic resolution can help identify neighborhood trends and areas of concern that may be less visible in coarser data.[4]

The Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry provides another example through its Environmental Justice Index.

ATSDR states that the EJI is calculated at census-tract level partly because environmental justice conditions can be highly localized. It warns that aggregation to larger areas can mask neighborhood-level variation.[5]

ATSDR specifically does not recommend aggregating or averaging EJI scores to estimate county or metropolitan-area scores because doing so can introduce error and misrepresent neighborhood-level social, environmental, and health conditions.[5]

This does not mean larger geographic statistics are inherently invalid.

They answer broader geographic questions.

## Temporal Aggregation Can Conceal Short Events

Aggregation across time produces a similar effect.

Suppose a system produces these daily observations:

`10, 10, 10, 50, 10, 10, 10`

The fourth day contains an obvious increase.

If the observations are reported only as a weekly average:

`15.7`

the short-duration peak is no longer directly visible.

The weekly average is mathematically valid.

It simply represents a different property of the observations.

CDC describes a comparable trade-off in public-health surveillance.

Combining data across multiple years can increase the stability of estimates based on small numbers, but it can also mask temporal trends occurring within the aggregated period.[1]

Aggregation can therefore improve one evidence property while weakening another.

`Greater Statistical Stability`

does not necessarily mean:

`Greater Temporal Resolution`

## Aggregation Can Hide Opposing Changes

Aggregation can also conceal changes that offset one another.

Consider:

| Location | Period 1 | Period 2 |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Area A | 20 | 40 |
| Area B | 60 | 40 |

At the local level:

- Area A increased from 20 to 40.
- Area B decreased from 60 to 40.

Now average the two areas.

| Period | Combined Average |
| --- | ---: |
| Period 1 | 40 |
| Period 2 | 40 |

The aggregate shows:

`No Change`

The underlying observations show:

`Increase in Area A`

and:

`Decrease in Area B`

Both representations are mathematically correct.

They answer different questions.

The aggregate answers:

**Did the combined average change?**

The detailed evidence answers:

**Did conditions within the component areas change?**

A stable aggregate therefore does not necessarily establish stable components.

## Averages Can Preserve the Center While Losing the Distribution

The same principle applies even without geography or time.

Consider two groups:

`Group A: 49, 50, 51`

`Group B: 10, 50, 90`

Both have an average of:

`50`

The average is accurate in both cases.

But the underlying distributions are very different.

The summary retains the central value.

It does not retain the amount or structure of variation around it.

This illustrates why a summary statistic should not automatically be interpreted as evidence that the underlying observations are uniform.

## Aggregation Can Be Necessary

The ability of aggregation to hide detail does not make aggregation inherently problematic.

Aggregation often serves legitimate purposes.

CDC's suicide-statistics example demonstrates one of them: estimates based on small numbers can be unstable, and combining observations can produce more stable estimates.[1]

The CDC sub-county surveillance research identifies another trade-off. Finer geographic data can improve local visibility, but small populations and small case counts can create both confidentiality and reliability problems.[3]

Aggregation may therefore be used to:

- Improve statistical stability.
- Protect confidentiality.
- Reduce noise.
- Support broader comparison.
- Simplify communication.
- Match the geographic or temporal scale of a research question.

The relevant evidence issue is not whether aggregation occurred.

It is whether material variation was lost for the claim being evaluated.

## What an Aggregate Does Not Establish

The absence of a visible pattern in aggregated evidence does not by itself establish that:

- No local variation exists.
- No subgroup differences exist.
- No short-duration event occurred.
- No extreme observations exist.
- Every component moved in the same direction.
- Conditions were uniform across the aggregated population.
- Conditions were stable throughout the aggregated period.
- The underlying distribution had little variation.
- A finer-resolution dataset would produce the same interpretation.

Likewise, finding a pattern after disaggregation does not automatically establish that the aggregate was incorrect.

The aggregate and the underlying observations may both be valid evidence.

They represent different levels of resolution.

## Why It Matters

Researchers frequently encounter evidence after aggregation has already occurred.

A table may contain annual rather than daily observations.

A map may show counties instead of neighborhoods.

A demographic table may combine several distinct populations.

A published statistic may contain only an average rather than the underlying distribution.

Once this happens, some distinctions in the original evidence may no longer be visible.

The narrower principle is:

**Failure to observe a pattern in aggregated evidence does not independently establish that the pattern is absent from the underlying observations.**

An aggregate can establish something about the combined system while remaining unable to establish what happened inside each component.

Evidence resolution therefore forms part of the meaning of the result.

## Limits

This brief addresses one narrow evidence issue: whether aggregation can conceal patterns that remain present at a finer observational level.

It does not establish that maximum resolution is always preferable.

More granular evidence can create its own limitations.

Small populations can produce unstable estimates.

Fine geographic or demographic resolution can increase confidentiality risk.

High-frequency measurements can contain noise.

Detailed evidence may be incomplete or inconsistent.

The public sources reviewed for this brief do not establish:

- That every aggregate conceals an important pattern.
- That disaggregation always changes the interpretation.
- That finer resolution automatically produces better evidence.
- That every local difference is substantively meaningful.
- That aggregated statistics are inherently misleading.
- That patterns visible at fine resolution can always be estimated reliably.
- That one level of aggregation is appropriate for every claim.

The appropriate resolution depends on what the evidence is being used to establish.

A national aggregate may be appropriate for a national question.

A county measure may be appropriate for a county question.

Neither automatically establishes what is occurring at a smaller scale.

The relevant distinction remains:

**Aggregation can preserve a valid summary while removing patterns present in the observations from which that summary was constructed.**
