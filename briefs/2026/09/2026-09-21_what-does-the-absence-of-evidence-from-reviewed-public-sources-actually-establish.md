# What Does the Absence of Evidence From Reviewed Public Sources Actually Establish?

**Date:** 21 September 2026  

**Domain:** Evidence Availability and Research Limits  

**Publication Type:** Brief

## Question

What may researchers legitimately conclude when expected information cannot be located in the public sources they reviewed?

## What the Evidence Shows

Failure to locate evidence in reviewed public sources establishes a finding about the reviewed evidence base.

It does not automatically establish that the underlying information, record, event, measurement, or documentation does not exist.

The central distinction is:

**Not Found in Reviewed Sources ≠ Does Not Exist**

PRISMA 2020 requires systematic reviews to identify the databases, registers, websites, organizations, reference lists, and other information sources searched or consulted, together with the date each source was last searched.[1]

Its expanded checklist also requires reporting the search strategies used for databases, registers, and websites, including material filters and limits.[1]

These requirements apply specifically to systematic-review reporting, but they illustrate an important evidentiary boundary:

A statement that something was "not found" becomes interpretable only in relation to the sources and search conditions under which it was sought.

A precise finding may therefore be:

**No qualifying public evidence was identified in the sources reviewed within the stated search scope.**

That is different from:

**No such evidence exists.**

The first describes a bounded research result.

The second makes a broader claim about the possible evidence universe.

## What "Not Found" Can Establish

When the relevant evidence boundary is stated, a negative search result can support narrower findings.

| Finding | What It Establishes |
| --- | --- |
| No record located in reviewed sources | The reviewed sources did not yield the specified record |
| No public disclosure located | The reviewed public material did not provide the expected disclosure |
| No qualifying measurement located | No measurement meeting the stated criteria was identified |
| No supporting study located | The reviewed search did not identify an eligible study supplying the specified evidence |
| No methodology located | The reviewed material did not disclose the methodology being sought |

These are findings about the evidence that was available and reviewed.

They are not automatically findings about information outside that boundary.

For example:

**The reviewed public materials do not disclose the methodology.**

does not mean:

**The organization has no methodology.**

A methodology could exist internally, in restricted material, in an unreviewed source, or in material unavailable to the researcher.

The absence of public evidence does not prove any of those alternatives either.

It preserves the distinction between what was verified and what remains unknown.

## Search Scope Changes the Meaning of a Negative Result

Cochrane guidance for systematic reviews states that searches should be as extensive as possible in order to reduce reporting bias and identify as much relevant evidence as possible. It also recommends consideration of multiple information sources, including relevant databases, registers, grey literature, previous reviews, and reference lists.[2]

A negative finding therefore needs to be interpreted in relation to the search boundary.

For example:

`Not found on one website`

and

`Not found after review of several relevant official repositories`

are both legitimate observations if accurately described.

They do not establish the same degree of public evidence availability.

A broader search can strengthen confidence that material was not located within the reviewed public evidence base.

It still does not automatically prove that the material does not exist anywhere.

## What Absence From Reviewed Sources Does Not Establish

Failure to locate evidence in reviewed public sources does not by itself establish that:

- The evidence does not exist.
- No record was ever created.
- No measurement was ever taken.
- An organization has no internal documentation.
- A private or restricted record does not exist.
- No other public source contains the material.
- The underlying event did not occur.
- The claim being investigated is false.
- The absence of disclosure was intentional.
- The information will never become public.

It also does not automatically establish that the search was inadequate.

A well-defined search can correctly conclude that specified evidence was not located within its scope.

The error occurs when that bounded finding is converted into a universal conclusion without additional evidence.

## Absence of Evidence and Evidence of Absence

Cochrane identifies a related distinction when interpreting research results.

Its Handbook warns against confusing "no evidence of an effect" with "evidence of no effect."[3]

The context is statistical evidence rather than public-source research, so the concepts should not be treated as identical.

But they illustrate the same narrower evidentiary principle:

**Failure to establish a proposition is not automatically equivalent to establishing its opposite.**

For public-source research:

`No Public Evidence Located`

should first be understood as:

`A finding about the reviewed public evidence base`

rather than automatically as:

`Proof of Non-Existence`

## Why It Matters

Research frequently needs to report what could not be verified.

That is useful information.

The risk arises when the wording becomes stronger than the evidence search supports.

Compare:

**Too broad:**  
"No methodology exists."

**Evidence-bounded:**  
"No methodology for the calculation was identified in the public sources reviewed."

The second statement preserves what the research actually established.

It identifies an evidence limitation without making an unsupported claim about internal, private, inaccessible, or undiscovered material.

The same distinction applies to datasets, filings, measurements, disclosures, technical documentation, and historical records.

The narrower rule is:

**Absence from reviewed public sources establishes absence from the reviewed public evidence base, subject to the stated search scope. It does not independently establish universal absence.**

## Limits

This brief addresses one narrow interpretation problem: what may be concluded when expected evidence cannot be located in reviewed public sources.

It does not establish that negative search results can never provide substantive evidence.

Under some research designs, non-observation can become informative when there are strong reasons to expect that evidence would have been observable if the underlying condition existed.

That stronger inference depends on the specific evidence environment, expected disclosure or detection conditions, coverage of the search, and plausible alternative explanations.

It should not be assumed merely because a public search returned no result.

The PRISMA and Cochrane materials cited here were developed for systematic reviews and health research. Their specific search procedures should not be treated as universal requirements for every public research project.

They nevertheless support narrower principles relevant to evidence interpretation:

- Search findings have defined boundaries.
- Search sources and dates affect interpretation.
- Search strategies and restrictions affect what can be located.
- Failure to identify evidence should not automatically be converted into evidence establishing the opposite proposition.

The public sources reviewed for this brief do not establish:

- A universal search procedure capable of proving non-existence.
- That every public research search must use the same sources.
- That comprehensive searching guarantees discovery of all existing evidence.
- That failure to find evidence necessarily reflects inadequate disclosure.
- That inaccessible or unindexed evidence can always be identified.
- That the same threshold for evidence of absence applies to every research question.

The appropriate conclusion should therefore remain bounded by what the reviewed evidence can establish.
