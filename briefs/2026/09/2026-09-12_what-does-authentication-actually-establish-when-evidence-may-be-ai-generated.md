# What Does Authentication Actually Establish When Evidence May Be AI-Generated?

**Date:** 12 September 2026

**Domain:** Evidence Authentication and AI-Generated Material

**Publication Type:** Brief

## Question

What does authentication actually establish when an item of evidence may have been generated, altered, or processed using artificial intelligence?

## What the Evidence Shows

Under Rule 901(a) of the U.S. Federal Rules of Evidence, authentication requires the proponent to produce evidence sufficient to support a finding that an item is what the proponent claims it is.[1]

That makes authentication claim-dependent.

The question is not simply whether an item is "real." The question is what the item is claimed to be and whether there is sufficient support for that claim.

This distinction becomes important when AI may be involved.

An item might be claimed to be:

- An original photograph of an event.
- An authentic recording captured by a particular device.
- An AI-enhanced version of an original recording.
- A synthetic image created for demonstrative purposes.
- Output produced by a specified AI system.
- A document whose content was partly generated or altered using AI.

Authentication addresses whether the evidence is what it is represented to be. It does not, by itself, resolve every other question about the evidentiary value of that material.

The following concepts are related but distinct:

| Concept | Core Question |
| --- | --- |
| Authenticity | Is the item what it is claimed to be? |
| Accuracy | Does the content correctly represent the relevant facts, event, data, or process? |
| Reliability | Can the source, method, or process be depended on for the purpose for which the evidence is offered? |
| Truth | Are the substantive factual propositions supported by the evidence actually true? |

These questions can overlap in practice, but they are not interchangeable.

The U.S. Advisory Committee on Evidence Rules has examined this distinction in the context of generative AI and deepfakes. In its May 2026 report, the Committee described deepfakes as raising an authenticity problem under Rule 901 and noted that deepfakes can be easy to generate and difficult to detect.[2]

The Committee was also working to develop possible rule language and included a working draft of a potential Rule 901(c). Under that draft, a party challenging an item as fabricated by generative AI would first need to present evidence sufficient to support a finding of fabrication. If that threshold were met, the proponent would then need to demonstrate to the court that the item was more likely than not authentic.[2]

The May 2026 report concluded that, at least at that time, an amendment to Rule 901 addressing deepfakes was not warranted. The Committee intended to continue refining possible rule language while holding the proposal in abeyance unless it later concluded that an amendment was necessary.[2]

A different procedural response appears in the Federal Court of Australia's April 2026 Generative Artificial Intelligence Practice Note. The practice note requires disclosure in specified circumstances when generative AI has been used in connection with evidentiary materials, including to create images, video recordings, sound recordings, or other multimedia presented to the Court, or where its use might reasonably be expected to affect the admissibility of the evidence or the use made of it by the Court.[3]

The practice note is not itself a rule of authentication. Its disclosure requirements address how generative AI was used in preparing material and help prevent the Court and other parties from being misled about that process.[3]

Disclosure of AI involvement therefore establishes information about provenance and preparation. It does not convert disclosure itself into proof that the resulting material is accurate, reliable, or true.

## What Authentication Does Not Establish

Authentication does not by itself establish that:

- The factual content of an item is accurate.
- Statements contained in an authentic document are true.
- A recording provides complete context.
- A process that generated the item is reliable for every purpose.
- An AI-generated item is false merely because AI was involved.
- An item is trustworthy merely because no AI involvement has been identified.
- Detection of AI involvement determines the weight that should be given to the evidence.
- Disclosure of AI use resolves every admissibility question.

A genuine document can contain a false statement.

An authentic recording can be incomplete or misleading without being fabricated.

An accurately identified AI-generated image can be authentic as an AI-generated image while still failing to establish that the depicted event occurred.

The reverse distinction also matters.

Showing that AI was involved in producing or altering an item establishes a provenance fact about how the material was created or processed. It does not, without additional evidence, establish whether the substantive content is accurate or whether the material should be relied upon for the proposition for which it is offered.

## Why It Matters

AI makes the distinction between origin and evidentiary value more important.

Authentication traditionally asks whether an item is what its proponent says it is. When synthetic generation, AI enhancement, automated transformation, or partial alteration may be involved, that question can require more precise description of the item's origin and processing history.

The central distinction is:

**Authenticity is not the same as accuracy, reliability, or truth.**

Identifying AI involvement can help establish provenance.

It can also identify a reason for closer examination.

But AI involvement is not, by itself, a complete evidentiary conclusion.

The evidentiary value of an item still depends on what the item is claimed to prove, how it was created or altered, what foundation supports it, and what other legal and factual questions remain.

## Limits

This brief uses public materials from the U.S. federal evidence-rule system and the Federal Court of Australia to examine the narrow question of authentication.

It does not survey every jurisdiction.

The sources reviewed show that courts and evidence-rule bodies are addressing AI-generated, AI-altered, and synthetic material, but they do not establish that all jurisdictions have adopted a common authentication standard for such evidence.

The possible U.S. Rule 901(c) discussed here was a working draft in the May 2026 Advisory Committee report. The Committee concluded at that time that an amendment to Rule 901 addressing deepfakes was not warranted and kept the possible rule under further study.[2]

The Australian practice note establishes disclosure requirements in specified circumstances. It is not itself an authentication rule and should not be treated as evidence that disclosure alone determines authenticity, admissibility, accuracy, reliability, or truth.[3]

The public sources reviewed for this brief do not establish:

- A universal legal definition of AI-generated evidence.
- A common cross-jurisdictional authentication threshold for synthetic evidence.
- A single technical method capable of detecting all AI-generated or AI-altered material.
- That proof of AI involvement determines the truth or falsity of the underlying content.
- That absence of detected AI involvement establishes authenticity.

The absence of a common standard in the public sources reviewed does not establish that individual courts, jurisdictions, or institutions lack additional rules, case law, procedures, or internal guidance.

It means that the reviewed evidence is not sufficient to support a claim of a uniform standard across jurisdictions.

This brief does not address fabricated legal citations or hallucinated authorities in legal filings as its primary issue. Those involve a different evidence and verification problem from the authentication of material offered as evidence.
