# Groundline-Public Publication Standard

**Project:** Groundline Research Lab

**Repository:** Groundline-Public

**Framework:** DGCP™

**Document Type:** Publication Governance

**Status:** Active

## 1. Purpose

This document defines the minimum standard required before material is published through `Groundline-Public`.

The publication process must preserve evidence quality, provenance, research status, public suitability, and traceability.

Public release is a governance decision.

It is not determined solely by whether a file is technically ready to commit.

## 2. Publication Gate

Before publication, a record should pass the following checks:

1. Evidence check.
2. Provenance check.
3. Source check.
4. Claim check.
5. Classification check.
6. Rights check.
7. Sensitive information check.
8. Public readability check.
9. Traceability check.
10. Final publication status check.

A material failure in any required check should prevent public release until resolved.

## 3. Evidence Check

The record should identify the evidence supporting its substantive claims.

Check whether:

• The evidence exists.

• The evidence is relevant to the claim.

• The evidence covers the stated scope.

• Important limitations are preserved.

• Contradictory evidence has been considered where materially relevant.

• Interpretation is distinguishable from source evidence.

A publication must not claim more than its evidence supports.

## 4. Provenance Check

The origin of material should be identifiable.

Where relevant, confirm:

• Source organization or author.

• Source title.

• Publication date.

• Retrieval date.

• Document or dataset identifier.

• Version.

• Canonical source location.

• Relevant transformation history.

Missing provenance that materially prevents verification should be resolved before publication.

## 5. Source Check

Sources should be evaluated according to their role.

Confirm whether a source is:

• Primary.

• Official.

• Secondary.

• Derived.

• Commentary.

• Dataset.

• Direct observation.

The publication must not misrepresent the status of a source.

Where a direct primary source is available and materially relevant, it should generally be preferred over indirect reporting.

## 6. Claim Check

Each material claim should remain within the available evidence.

Check for accidental conversion of:

• Announcement into execution.

• Plan into completion.

• Approval into implementation.

• Availability into use.

• Access into ownership.

• Estimate into measurement.

• Correlation into causation.

• Partial coverage into complete representation.

• Absence of evidence into evidence of absence.

• Model output into direct observation.

Qualification should be retained where necessary.

## 7. Research State Check

Confirm that the publication distinguishes relevant research states.

These may include:

**Observation:** What was found or recorded.

**Evidence:** What supports or constrains the observation.

**Structure:** How components or relationships are organized.

**Interpretation:** What the evidence may mean.

**Hypothesis:** What remains to be tested.

**Conclusion:** What can reasonably be supported within the stated scope.

A hypothesis must not be published as a verified conclusion.

## 8. Classification Check

Confirm that the material belongs in the public repository.

Possible public classifications include:

• Article.

• Brief.

• Explainer.

• Public research record.

• Dataset.

• Methodology note.

• Evidence index.

• Educational material.

Internal or restricted material must not be reclassified as public merely for convenience.

## 9. Rights Check

Confirm that publication does not improperly redistribute third party material.

Review:

• Copyright.

• Dataset terms.

• Publication licenses.

• Image rights.

• Software licenses.

• Quotation limits.

• Redistribution restrictions.

• Attribution requirements.

Evidence may be valid for research use without being suitable for republication.

## 10. Sensitive Information Check

Review the record for information that should not be publicly released.

This includes, where applicable:

• Personal information.

• Authentication credentials.

• Confidential information.

• Restricted material.

• Security sensitive operational details.

• Legally protected information.

• Private research notes.

• Internal identifiers not intended for public release.

Sensitive material should be removed, generalized, or retained outside the public repository as appropriate.

## 11. Data Check

Where a publication contains data, confirm:

1. Units are defined.
2. Dates are clear.
3. Geographic scope is clear.
4. Missing values are handled explicitly.
5. Transformations are documented where material.
6. Source data and derived data are distinguishable.
7. Calculations can be understood or reproduced where practical.
8. Dataset limitations are stated.

Data should not be presented without enough context to interpret it correctly.

## 12. Public Readability Check

The publication should be understandable to its intended public audience.

Review:

• Definitions.

• Terminology.

• Sentence clarity.

• Necessary context.

• Structure.

• Unsupported jargon.

• Ambiguous wording.

Simplification must not weaken research integrity.

## 13. Traceability Check

Where appropriate, the public record should maintain a traceable relationship to supporting material.

Traceability may include:

• Source citations.

• Record identifiers.

• Dataset references.

• Methodology references.

• Related public records.

• Correction history.

• Version relationships.

Internal material does not need to be exposed merely to demonstrate traceability.

## 14. Duplicate Check

Before publishing a new record, check whether substantially equivalent public material already exists.

A new publication may still be appropriate when it:

• Adds new evidence.

• Changes scope.

• Updates an earlier record.

• Corrects earlier information.

• Serves a different educational purpose.

Where appropriate, related records should reference one another.

## 15. Correction Check

If the publication replaces or corrects an earlier record, identify the relationship explicitly.

Possible states include:

• Correction.

• Update.

• Expansion.

• Superseding record.

• Methodology revision.

Substantive historical differences should remain traceable.

## 16. Publication Status

A record may move through states such as:

**Working:** Not for public release.

**Review:** Evidence or publication review in progress.

**Public Ready:** Required checks completed.

**Published:** Released through Groundline-Public.

**Corrected:** A material correction has been issued.

**Superseded:** A later record replaces the current analytical or evidentiary state.

**Archived:** Retained for historical traceability.

Only material at an approved public state should be committed as a substantive publication to `Groundline-Public`.

## 17. Final Public Check

Before final release, confirm:

• The title is correct.

• The date is correct.

• The repository path is correct.

• The classification is correct.

• Evidence is sufficient.

• Sources are traceable.

• Claims are proportionate.

• Rights have been considered.

• No restricted information remains.

• The record is suitable for permanent public preservation.

## 18. Permanent Record Principle

Material published through `Groundline-Public` should be treated as part of the public research record.

Corrections and updates should therefore be made transparently rather than through silent substantive rewriting.

Public preservation should support later verification of what was known, published, and concluded at a given time.
